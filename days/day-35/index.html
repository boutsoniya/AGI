Can an AI know when it doesn't know?

An answer is not always the best response. Sometimes the most intelligent move is to recognise missing evidence and stop.

uncertaintycalibrationreliability
THE IDEA

A model's uncertainty is not the same as the uncertainty expressed in its words. A system can generate “I'm 90% sure” without that number being calibrated. Calibration asks whether, across many predictions made at a given confidence level, roughly that proportion is correct. Selective prediction goes one step further: a system can answer when its evidence is strong and abstain when it is not.

WHY IT MATTERS

In high-stakes or changing situations, an unsupported answer can be worse than no answer. Knowing when to defer, ask a question, retrieve evidence or hand off to a human is part of reliable intelligence—not a weakness to hide.

TRY THIS

Make a set of 20 questions: some you know, some obscure, and some whose answer depends on recent information. Ask an AI to answer each, assign confidence, and flag which it would abstain from. Verify the answers. Group the results by confidence and see whether the high-confidence group is actually more accurate.

WHERE IT BREAKS

A model may use confident language without calibrated confidence. It can also abstain too often, avoiding easy questions, or too rarely, bluffing when evidence is weak. Calibration can shift when the topic or data changes.

TAKEAWAY

A trustworthy system does not only answer well. It knows when its evidence is not enough.

BEFORE YOU READ

When an AI has weak evidence, what should it do?

A. Give its best guess confidently

B. State uncertainty, seek evidence or abstain

Answer: B. Uncertainty should guide the next action—retrieve, clarify, defer or abstain.
