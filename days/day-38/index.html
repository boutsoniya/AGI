Can multiple AI agents solve a problem together?

Giving a task to five agents does not automatically give you a better team.

multi-agent systemscoordinationcollaboration
THE IDEA

A multi-agent system contains two or more agents that interact while pursuing goals. They may divide a task, share information, negotiate, compete or check one another's work. The difficult part is coordination: each agent has only a partial view, may use different assumptions, and can take actions that affect the others. A shared goal does not guarantee a shared understanding of how to reach it.

WHY IT MATTERS

Teams of specialised agents can tackle work that is too broad for one process: one can gather evidence, another analyse it, and another verify the result. But adding agents also adds communication overhead, duplicated work and new ways for errors to spread. The design question is not 'how many agents?' but 'what should each agent own, know and be allowed to do?'

TRY THIS

Ask three AI agents to plan a small college event. Give one the budget, one the venue constraints and one the attendee needs. First let them work independently, then make them exchange their findings and produce one plan. Check whether the final plan respects all three constraint sets, whether any information was lost, and whether one agent simply dominated the decision.

WHERE IT BREAKS

Agents can agree because they share the same mistaken assumption, endlessly debate, duplicate tasks or pass an error from one to another. More messages do not necessarily mean better coordination. A reliable system needs clear roles, shared state, conflict resolution and a final independent check.

TAKEAWAY

A team of agents becomes useful when coordination makes their combined work more reliable than isolated work.

BEFORE YOU READ

Three agents produce conflicting event plans. What should the system do?

A. Let the loudest or fastest agent decide

B. Compare evidence against shared constraints and resolve conflicts

Answer: B. Shared constraints and traceable evidence provide a principled way to reconcile different proposals.
